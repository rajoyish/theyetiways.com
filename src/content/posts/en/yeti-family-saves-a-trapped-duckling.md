The afternoon had gone soft and honey-coloured over the courtyard. I had the
water bucket on my hip for the trough, Papa Yeti had his old walking staff, and
Babu Yeti was walking between us the way he does when he is pretending not to
hold anyone's paw.

Over by the hand cart, a duckling the size of a momo was losing a fight with a
piece of straw. Babu Yeti pointed. We all smiled. It was that kind of day.

## The wind had other plans

Then a cloud took the sun, and a gust came across the courtyard hard enough to
rock the cart. The little clay brazier beside the wheel tipped off its stone.
Thud. Coals across the gravel. Then the dry crackle that every Yeti on a
mountain feels in the pit of the stomach.

The straw caught. Low flames curled into a ring around the base of the wheel,
and the duckling backed up against the spokes in the middle of it, wings tucked
tight. Its mother quacked at the edge. Its sibling peeped from the other side.
Nobody with feathers could do a thing.

We ran, and then we stopped short at the edge of the ring, all three of us,
staring.

## Three paws, three jobs

Babu Yeti moved first. He set his brow, pointed straight at the duckling, and
turned his whole head to Papa Yeti. No words. He did not need any.

Papa Yeti planted his feet and swung that staff in one flat circle at knee
height. Whoosh. Burning straw and coals went skittering out across the gravel
in a spray of sparks, and the ring broke open into little heaps of smoking
straw.

Then it was my turn, and I had the bucket. Hiss. The biggest cloud of steam I
have ever made on purpose. When it cleared, the ground around the wheel was
dark and wet and safe, and the duckling sat in the middle of it looking deeply
offended about the bath.

Babu Yeti knelt and laid one paw flat in front of it. The duckling hopped right
over his paw and ran to its family, and they bundled together under the mother
duck's wing.

And Papa Yeti, the hero of the courtyard, rubbed the back of his neck and went
shy. I watched it happen. The cheeks went up, the eyes went down, and he gave
the ducks a small nod like a Yeti apologising for taking up space.

> He had just broken a ring of fire with a stick, and the hardest part for him
> was being looked at.

## The Yeti Way

**A rescue is a family sharing the work, and nobody has to be the whole hero.**

Babu Yeti had the eyes. Papa Yeti had the reach. I had the water. Any one of us
alone would have stood at the edge of that ring and worried. Together, it was
over before the kettle at home could have boiled.

That is also why Papa Yeti could stop looking at his feet. The thanks was never
only his to carry. Let your family split the credit the way they split the
work, and being thanked gets much easier.

We walked home under the walnut tree with an empty bucket, a sooty staff, and
Babu Yeti waving goodbye to the ducks over his shoulder the whole way. ❄️❤️

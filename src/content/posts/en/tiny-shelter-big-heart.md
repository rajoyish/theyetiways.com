I was lying on my stomach in the moss, which is my favourite way to be in a
forest, watching the ants go home.

They walk in a line. Nobody tells them to. They cross the moss, take the strip
of dry dirt, and vanish into a hole the size of my nose. The sun was warm on my
back and I had my face a paw's width from the line and no plans at all.

## The first drop

Then something the size of an ant's whole house landed next to the hole. Smack.
Water went up in a little crown. Dirt went everywhere.

The ants froze with their feelers up. Two more drops came down, wet circles on
the dry path, and the line broke into a scramble. I looked up. The sun was gone,
the sky had gone grey behind the leaves, and thunder rolled over the trees like
somebody moving furniture upstairs.

I know what rain does to a hole the size of my nose.

## The roof that wanted to slide

There were two big brown leaves by the ferns. I grabbed both, ran back on all
fours, and leaned them together over the trail in a little peaked roof. It
looked very good for about one breath.

Then the real downpour started. Water pooled on the leaves and the whole roof
began sliding toward the ants, so I put both paws flat on the edges and held.
Rain poured over my paws. I made a noise I am not proud of. With one paw still
on the roof, I found a dry twig, jammed it under the ridge, and felt it snap
into place. The roof stood on its own.

Compassion begins the moment we kneel down to the earth and realize that every
heartbeat, however tiny, shares our fragile journey home. I did not have that
sentence yet. I only had mud on my knees and a line of ants going home dry.

> True strength is not measured by the storms we can endure alone, but by the
> gentle roofs we build to keep smaller souls dry.

I put my chin down on the wet moss and looked in. They were marching again, neat
as ever, feelers bobbing. Nobody said thank you. Ants don't. So I stood up in the
rain, threw my arms wide, and got soaked on purpose, because somebody had to
celebrate.

## The Yeti Way

**No life is too small to deserve shelter from the storm, and no act of
kindness is too quiet to change the world for the one who receives it.**

The ants will never know my name. They won't remember the roof, and the next
wind will take the leaves. That is fine. For one afternoon their whole world
stayed dry, and I did it with two leaves and a stick.

When the sun came back I sat by the shelter and watched the last of them go in.
Next to an ant, I am enormous. That turns out to be useful for exactly this:
getting down low and holding something over the smaller ones until the weather
passes.

Look down more often. Somebody small is usually getting rained on. ❄️❤️
